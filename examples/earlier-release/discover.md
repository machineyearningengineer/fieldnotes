## Direct Evidence and Technological Status of Sodium-Ion Batteries

Sodium-ion battery technology is approaching the energy density performance of commercial lithium-ion batteries and is projected to achieve lower prices as development by academic groups and start-up companies progresses, although its long-term lifespan requires further improvement\. [1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04963)

Conventional sodium-ion cells utilize insertion and de-insertion positive electrode materials rather than conversion chemistries, exhibiting theoretical values of approximately 3\.3 V for average positive electrode voltage versus Na/Na\+, around 170 mAh/g specific capacity, approximately 760 mAh/cm3 capacity density, and theoretical gravimetric and volumetric energy densities of roughly 560 Wh/kg and 2500 Wh/L, respectively\. [2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04391)

## Gaps in Grid Storage Evidence for Sodium-Ion Technology

The retrieved literature does not evaluate sodium-ion batteries in stationary grid deployments or power-system operational models; instead, grid-level evaluations focus on alternative storage chemistries such as lithium-ion, vanadium redox flow, lead-acid, and sodium-sulphur systems\. [3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00486)

Assessing post-lithium-ion battery technologies requires resolving challenges related to end-of-life energy density, interface stability, degradation mechanisms, and long-term cyclability under varied operational conditions\. [1](https://arxiv.org/abs/2005.04963)

## Context from Related Sodium Chemistries and Grid Requirements

Related sodium conversion chemistries, specifically sodium-sulphur and sodium-oxygen, are considered attractive for stationary grid storage due to the natural abundance of sulphur and oxygen and high theoretical energy densities, but high-temperature sodium-sulphur variants suffer from safety, corrosion, and parasitic heating demands that reduce overall efficiency to 87%\. [2](https://arxiv.org/abs/2505.04391)

Grid-connected battery applications require overcoming wear costs driven by cycle life and replacement intervals, where wear costs reflect the initial capital expenditure amortized over the equivalent full cycles delivered before reaching degradation thresholds\. [3](https://arxiv.org/abs/2007.00486)
